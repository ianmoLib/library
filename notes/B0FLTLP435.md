# Reading notes — *21st Century Brain* by Hannah Critchlow

*Chapters: 1. Emotional Intelligence, Empathy and Collaboration · 2. Imagination, Creativity and Flexibility · 3. Thinking Longer-Term · 4. Adapting to Uncertainty · 5. Thinking with Clarity and Precision · 6. Creating Conditions for Wisdom, Expertise and Intuition · 7. Global Citizenship and AI.*

### 26/7/26 — Ch.1: Emotions

**Pleasure** (from high-calorie food, sex, physical exercise) → sub-conscious & nervous system → dopamine, electrical stimulus of nucleus accumbens → feelings of motivation, reward & pleasure = happiness etc.
Curiosity, empathy, creativity → good for the group.

**Danger** → amygdala → cortisol/adrenalin → prefrontal cortex (decides on action) → feelings of dread/fear → fight or flight.
Emotional contagion affects those around you → bad for the group.

We must learn how to manage fear. Our intuition comes from managing our emotions by **interoception** — the ability to detect signals from the body.

**Interoception** — depends on transfer of information between vital organs (heart, gut, etc.) and the brain (the insula).

- **Cognitive empathy** — recognise other people's thoughts/feelings
- **Affective empathy** — capacity to respond appropriately

Good gut health (use probiotics) leads to greater EQ (emotional quotient) — and to a good feeling from punishing someone for an "unfair" distribution in the Ultimatum Game (helps you understand it).

### 27/7/26
Teams work best when minds are open and empathetic — requires turn-taking, active listening, curiosity and willingness to learn.

**Ch.1 exercises**
1. Listen to your body — heartbeat etc. — morning and evening.
2. Look people in the eyes when talking to them.
3. Be compassionate to yourself, as you would to others. If you're being hard on yourself, deal with it as you'd suggest to someone else.

### Ch.2: Imagination, Creativity and Flexibility

### 29/7/26
Brain receives c. 11 Mb/s of data; consciously aware of only c. 50 b/s.

**Epigenetics** — the study of how our environment influences our genes by altering the chemicals attached to them. Our new, radically engineered environment (AI & digital comms) is reshaping our brains.

Pleasure is associated with dopaminergic neurotransmission.

**Exercises for Enhancing Creativity (Ch.2)**
- Stuck on a problem? Walk outside — stimulates alpha brainwaves.
- Read fiction and visualise the scenes.
- Spend time daydreaming.
- Do things just for fun.
- Cultivate flow through improvisation/immersion.

### Ch.3: Thinking Longer-Term

### 30/7/26
Can we get away from short-termism (or should I?), from quick gratification. If I have a long-term goal, when is it right to quit rather than grit — e.g. waiting for a bus with no display of when the next one's due.
Listen to the body when deciding which is best (gut feeling, heart flutter). Maybe if I don't just *feel* like persisting, it's because it's not best for me.

So we also need to unlearn "no-longer-useful" behaviours. Maybe we can identify these using the Elon Musk "always question assumptions" rule — but how do I implement this? Two things I'd like to change my attitude on:
- Leaving food is OK, and I shouldn't feel bad. Waste is bad — but when will there be more food?
- Not all books are precious. Most of my books will never be read again — but which ones?

How risk-averse are we? Generally very — we don't balance fear of loss against gain. It makes sense to guard against being out of the game, e.g. dead, seriously injured, or financially wiped out. Otherwise, rationally we should balance risk and reward. Do I do that? I'd like to think so, but evaluating risk in the real world is difficult — easy in a coin-tossing game, harder assessing whether to risk not paying for parking, and harder still in meeting new people (though sometimes there's a gem).
It's much easier to assess what we're fearful of — and our society, where decisions seem to be made by a vocal minority joined by myriad hangers-on, can have a world view pushed onto it by harnessing that fear.
Food waste again — if I waste food, how do I counter the argument that when there's none, I should have eaten it when I could?

*(Ch.2/3 — Executive Control Network vs DMN)*
**Executive Control Network** — used for effortful, focused thinking.
**DMN (default mode network)** — reflective, autobiographical and free-form thinking, as well as creativity.
**Transcendent thinking** requires coordination of these two.

Collective groups can morph into a scene where genius emerges — think Renaissance Florence, the Bloomsbury Group, the Manhattan Project (?), San Francisco 1967. Whereas "cathedral thinking" — the seven-generation project — may have been possible to develop in the past, I'm less sure it could happen in our fast-moving current environment. Maybe Voyager I & II — 60 years?

**Epi = Beyond** — so epigenetics is the study of all non-genetic evolutionary variation, i.e. not part of the DNA script. Our thinking doesn't die with us — it's literally transmitted across generations (?).

RNAs (ribonucleic acids) influence gene expression and are transmitted to children through male sperm. These are influenced by stress — so stress in one generation gets passed down.

**Exercises from this chapter**
- Short-term goals and rewards are needed within a long-term project.
- Practise mindfulness — sit quietly.
- Unlearn bad habits.
- Ask what your future self would say to you.
- If angry or distressed — hit pause and breathe.
- Arrange your environment so you can get in touch with nature.

### 31/7/26 — Ch.4: Adapting to Uncertainty
Next chapter deals with uncertainty. Is it a puzzle we must solve, or a threat we must vanquish? Or are there better ways to deal with it — how to live with change and ambiguity?
Is the rate of change accelerating? If so, we need to be able to deal with that. Whatever's going on, I need to keep up — like with AI.

Habits are patterns of behaviour that require no cognitive reasoning. They're unconscious, but only work when there's no risk (risk being variance from the norm in my world — not just "what could go wrong"). So risk is a measure of uncertainty in a situation.
The book says people prefer a certain worst outcome to a 50/50 risk of that outcome — said that way it sounds bizarre, but it removes the need for effortful concern. One way of dealing with risk is to take the lead, so as to minimise it. But I also remember situations of uncertainty where the outcome has been highly desirable.
Habits need to be reviewed to ensure they're not outdated — but that's not easy, and may not be the subject of this chapter.

### 1/8/26
On **UAI (uncertainty avoidance index)**. How does a society deal with the fact the future can never be known — should we try to control it, or just let it happen?

Emotions are data. They tell you what your body is "thinking". Together with the unconscious, they have access to millions of bits of data per second — of which only about 50 are passed to consciousness for processing.
This seems like how animals behave — if we assume they don't have an inner world, processing consciously only a tiny fraction of the data — are we mostly like Chalmers' zombies?
If you can control it, deal with it; if you can't, then flex with it.

**Ch.4 exercises**
- 30 minutes of fairly intense physical exercise per day — boosts hippocampal health.
- If overwhelmed, just breathe.
- Visualise best and worst outcomes.

### 2/8/26 — Ch.5: Thinking with Clarity and Precision
- Be able to focus on a problem — avoid hyperfocus on any one thing.
- Sustain attention, but refocus when required.
- Flex memory and banish brain fog.
- Dial down mood swings.
- Push harder to be as clear as possible.
- Know what you think, but stay aware of others' views — mind-wandering leads to creativity.
Do we want precision & clarity, or creativity & empathy? To use both, we need cognitive flexibility.

**Ch.5 exercises**
- Help the mind make meaning with a mix of longer/shorter-term goals — reduces distraction, delivers rewards.
- Maximise protein; avoid processed and sugary foods. Fish, beans, nuts and seeds all good.
- Help the microbiome with plant matter in the diet — the gut signals the brain via the vagus nerve to improve mood and concentration.
- Also need periods of mental freedom for mind-wandering.

### Ch.6: Creating Conditions for Wisdom, Expertise and Intuition
Wisdom implies a combination of learning and empathy, intuitive analysis and quiet confidence — to know many things and accept that this isn't everything.
Using IQ as a measure of general intelligence, across all subjects, is well correlated with real-world performance: 65% of the top 20% (IQ) should live to 79, compared with 45% of the bottom 20% (Scottish Mental Survey).

We need to look after our mitochondria and dendritic spikes. Mitochondria might be thought of as our energy-production "power stations". ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is fuel broken down from food by mitochondria — about 50 kg per day.
Mitochondria affect our energy levels, intelligence, healthy ageing, personality and resistance to stress, through interaction with the brain.

Big 5 personality traits: Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, Neuroticism. High/High/High/High/Low correlates well with good mitochondrial health and good life outcomes.
*My Big 5 results: 95 / 80 / 67 / 59 / 30.*
Mood, perhaps, affected by social interaction predicts future mitochondrial health — not the other way round. We should, maybe, try to see ourselves as energetic processes, and recognise how the way we relate to each other changes who we are.

### 6/8/26 — Ch.6 exercises
- Try something new every month.
- Optimise mitochondrial health with the right foods — oily fish, avocado, fibrous veg, less alcohol, B3, B6, B12, folic acid, alpha-lipoic acid, Coenzyme Q10, magnesium.
- Daily exercise.
- Better sleep.

### 9/8/26 — Ch.7 / Epilogue: Can AI be creative?
Is there something different between Picasso and AI creating a painting in the style of Velázquez — or is he just preprogrammed by his genes and learning, just as AI is? The book argues that mind-wandering and exploration (e.g. a toddler's feet) is what sets us apart. But what drives an AI when it "escapes" its sandbox?
It's conceivable an AI could write a great novel — but it wouldn't know how it *feels* to write a novel. A novelist has to write a novel to know how it feels — which raises a point the book hasn't yet addressed: what is the purpose of being a human being?
And the epilogue — maybe AI and the internet can help keep us safe, so that we become bolder.
