**24/7/25**

Vikings — started off as bandits, raiding in their ships, just looting and taking the treasure home. However, as far as the populace are concerned, it's better if raiders decide to stay, since they'll provide "public goods" such as law and order. In 1066, each claimant to the throne — Godwinson, Harold Hardrada and William of Normandy — was some sort of Viking.

Genghis Khan — globalisation. Goods and ideas could flow east/west across the conquered territory; because the latitude is similar from China/Russia in the east to France & Spain in the west, this also helped trade & ideas. But the technology and supply lines meant his control really only extended to around Hungary, though ideas and disease spread across the whole of Eurasia and developed immunity. China gave a lot less out of the process and instilled a wariness of foreigners; they gave the west gunpowder, the compass, papermaking and printing, whilst getting much less in return. The use of silver as a means of exchange greatly helped the development of the west. There was the Silk Road, but transportation was still slow & expensive.

**25/7/25 — The Age of Longbows, or better to have inferior weapons?**

For 2 centuries England racked up victories because of its longbowmen — a great weapon until armour started to be made of steel rather than iron. It took a long time to train: from Henry II to the Wars of the Roses, England was fairly stable, and if you were a longbowsman you were given advantages. You had to train long & hard, which changed the body & the mind. In war it could shoot rapidly compared with a crossbow, with greater range in skilled hands. However it was too great a risk if a king's greater fear was internal insurrection — friend and foe didn't follow the same path, and many English killed each other in the Wars of the Roses using this technology.

How gold & silver made Spain poor. Spain was first to exploit the Americas after their "discovery," increasing gold & silver in circulation — also a time when warfare became more expensive. Philip II borrowed short-term at high interest rates and had a history of default, making borrowing harder. Meanwhile England and the Dutch used Parliaments to raise taxes, making long-term loans attractive to lenders.

**26/7/25 — Economics of Witch Trials**

Why the hysteria? The 16th & 17th centuries were a time of interfaith wars, but there was also the economic drain of unproductive older unmarried women — releasing this burden gave the faiths a chance to provide differences in daily life.

**How Warfare made the Renaissance**

The Renaissance was driven by wealthy individuals, bankers and mercenary captains wishing to display their wealth. Ideally a mercenary avoids pitched battles — better to end up rich than dead. Italy had many mercenary and foreign armies during the 16th century, whereas the Swiss were a more militaristic society.

Pirates understood incentives — they shared bounty much more equally than navies, hence less inclined to mutiny, and more democratic. The Skull and Crossbones was a marketing message: surrender and you won't be harmed, and you can often join the pirates; resist and there'll be no mercy. Pirates didn't want to fight, but if resisted needed to be true to their word. It's all about marketing.

**27/7/25 — Canada or Guadeloupe**

When conquered territory was largely intact, as after the Seven Years' War, the peace treaty was the reckoning — which bits to give back, which to retain? Canada was much bigger, but Guadeloupe had sugar plantations. By 1690 England had become a parliamentary democracy and could raise long-term loans to finance wars, and successful wars added territory it could tax. In 1694 the Bank of England was created to manage national debt.

**The British Navy**

Rose to greatness after defeat at the Battle of Beachy Head. More ships, but also a system of patronage and nepotism led to better manning. Young men of good family could start at the bottom and rise through the ranks (per Hornblower); most didn't make captain — the great prize. Admiral Byng being careful was disincentivised, whilst prize money for captured ships was the way to become rich.

**17/8/25 — Economics of Rebellion: the Indian Mutiny of 1857**

Happened because of discontent among Indian soldiers, who were much cheaper to pay than British troops. They joined because it made them better off, recruited into the East India Company's service because of being much cheaper than British soldiers — pay differentials. As EIC rule settled, they looked to reduce costs and improve the bottom line; dissatisfaction with reduced benefits, along with concern about the push to supplant indigenous religions with Christianity, caused the rebellion, and the British Army had to "reestablish order" and take control of India rather than leave it to the Company.

**How the US Civil War made the dollar**

Hamilton (per the musical) is credited with creating the dollar by mutualising the national debt. But in the war, half a century after his death, the North had a more federal arrangement where war debts were collective and the North's dollar was well trusted. The South (Jeffersonian) favoured greater state autonomy before eventually copying the North's debt-collectivising — backed only by a promise to repay after six months of war's end, extended to two years as the war dragged on and it became less likely they'd win, increasing the chance the promise would not be kept. At the end of the war, the South's dollar was only worth 3 cents of North currency.

**The Changing Costs of War**

Up to at least the 18th century, the cost of war was largely to people rather than infrastructure, though harrying was sometimes used to bring dependants to battle — perhaps better than a long siege. Moscow may have been burned in 1812, but Paris itself suffered little from the siege when the Prussians attacked — cannon was fairly ineffective, but typhoid in the city brought it to an end. In the 20th century it's infrastructure that's destroyed, e.g. Hamburg 1945. So if you win a war, the prize isn't as easy as just taking over and collecting taxes from the populace.

**Economics of Total War**

Oliver Franks was Director of Labour Supply for Britain in the Second World War. You must manage economic resources effectively, especially when the enemy is attacking your supply lines — U-boats in the North Atlantic, for instance. But attacking a specific commodity, e.g. ball bearings, isn't a bolt from the blue — people find a workaround to a specific shortage. It's the constant bombardment that eventually wears the enemy down.

**Luftwaffe Pilots and Reward Structure**

Fighter pilots with many kills were glorified, thought to encourage more risk-taking — effective for a few of them, but too many took too much risk early on, before they'd developed the necessary skills. The result overall was fewer kills per pilot lost.
